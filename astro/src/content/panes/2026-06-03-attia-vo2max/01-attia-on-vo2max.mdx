# 🫀 Peter Attia & VO2max

<Callout type="info" title="Quick orientation">
<strong>Peter Attia</strong> is a physician focused on longevity ("Medicine 3.0"),
author of <em>Outlive</em> (2023) and host of <em>The Drive</em> podcast.
<strong>VO2max</strong> is the maximum rate your body can use oxygen during
all-out exercise — the single best lab measure of cardiorespiratory fitness.
Attia treats it as one of the most powerful, trainable predictors of how long and
how well you'll live.
</Callout>

## Who Attia is

- Stanford MD, surgical training at Johns Hopkins, ex-McKinsey — now centered on
  **lifespan + healthspan** (how long you live *and* how long you stay capable).
- His framework leans hard on exercise as the highest-leverage longevity tool,
  with VO2max and strength/stability as the headline metrics.

## What VO2max actually is

- **Definition:** max oxygen consumption, in **mL of O₂ per kg per minute**
  (mL/kg/min). The aerobic ceiling.
- **Physiology:** set by how much oxygenated blood the heart pumps (cardiac
  output) × how well muscles extract that oxygen (a-vO₂ difference). Both improve
  with training.
- **Measured** properly via a graded test to exhaustion with a metabolic cart
  (CPET); estimated less precisely by wearables and submaximal tests.

## Why Attia fixates on it

<Callout type="success" title="The mortality argument">
Cardiorespiratory fitness tracks with all-cause mortality more strongly than most
classic risk factors. Attia frequently cites large cohort data (e.g. Mandsager et
al., <em>JAMA Network Open</em> 2018, ~122,000 patients) where the least-fit had
dramatically higher death rates than the fittest — a gap he argues rivals or
exceeds the risk from smoking, diabetes, or hypertension.
</Callout>

Two ideas do most of the work:

1. **The biggest gains are at the bottom.** Moving from the *least fit* quartile
   to merely *below-average* yields the largest relative drop in mortality risk.
   "The cost of doing nothing" is highest for the unfit.
2. **VO2max is a reserve you spend over decades.** It declines roughly ~10% per
   decade after your 30s and faster late in life. Build a high ceiling now and you
   keep functional independence later — his "**centenarian decathlon**": list the
   physical things you want to do in your final decade, back-calculate the fitness
   required, and train for it today.

## How he says to train it

<Mermaid chart={`flowchart LR
  Z["Zone 2 base<br/>~3-4 hrs/week<br/>conversational-but-labored"] --> CAP["raises the floor:<br/>mitochondria, fat oxidation"]
  V["VO2max intervals<br/>~1x/week<br/>e.g. Norwegian 4x4"] --> CEIL["raises the ceiling:<br/>peak aerobic power"]
  CAP --> FIT["higher, more durable VO2max"]
  CEIL --> FIT
`} />

- **Zone 2** (most of the volume): steady aerobic work at a pace you can barely
  hold a conversation through — builds the aerobic base.
- **VO2max intervals** (about once a week): hard efforts near max, classically the
  **4×4** — 4 minutes near-max, 4 minutes easy, repeated ~4 times.

<Callout type="warning" title="Accuracy caveat">
The framing here is solid and reflects Attia's well-known positions, but the
specific numbers — hazard ratios, the exact VO2max bands, the precise decline
rate — are from memory and rounded. Treat them as directional. For anything you'd
act on or quote, check <em>Outlive</em> or the primary studies directly, and get a
real test rather than trusting an estimate.
</Callout>
